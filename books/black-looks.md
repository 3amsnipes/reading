The early onset of this book reminds me of this quote from Walter Mosley, 

> If we are not in literature, we do not exist.
— Walter Mosley
> 

In this intro Bell Hooks takes it a step further, expanding beyond the spaces of written literature but media interpretation overall—with an increased emphasis on film specifically. She’s speaking on representation with intention. 

We have to show up in spaces—be seen, be heard. Yet we have to do so in a way that’s enlightening. Simply showing up—making movies, publishing novels, creating stories—is not enough.  We should be conscious of how we’re showing up in media. She alludes to how media interpretation is linked to how we view ourselves, how we think and show up. 

If we change images without shifting thinking we have done nothing.



#### Loving Blackness as Political Resistance


The great Kendrick Lamar once said “I love myself”

One of the reasons I have “i” tattooed on me, not simply because the Kendrick Lamar (who is the greatest rapper of all time—a conversation that gets very little debate) but it’s for what the song represents. 

<img width="300" height="300" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/665b6666-f909-4d08-8075-47d51f368abf" />

*Peak musical experience* 

This released in Highschool and as a lead single there was literally a myriad of black kids saying I love myself. Through a catchy tune and amazing beat young boys and girls from the other side of the country I love myself  rang through the halls. It was cool to see—even at the time. 

<img width="220" height="166" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/30c2af33-ba3f-4847-bc6b-d2b84ce96289" />

There’s power in words and how you see yourself is linked to those words. How you engage with the world, your community, the people around you, is tied to the words you say—and believe—about yourself. You must strip yourself of negative connotations and self-hatred before any of this work is done. 

Bell Hooks echoes this message (I guess it’s probably more fitting to say Kendrick is echoing her message) throughout this chapter. This idea that no we are not all the same, yet we cannot see ourselves through our flaws and shortcomings. The black experience and the white experience are different, but that gap isn’t a shortcoming. That gap *is* the black culture—and it’s empowering.

Though the struggle lies in the fact that most of the self-hatred outlined is not a conscious line of thought. It’s the unconscious way white washed perspectives seep into the patterns of the world. Wether that’s through disliking darker skin or likening weaves vs natural hair. These societal norms impact our self image—especially as children. The issue becomes in the fact that we’ve tattooed those ideas as permanent in our minds. 

There’s no one-all fix for that. It’s showing up and changing that narrative—within ourselves first—and from there I believe the seed grows.
